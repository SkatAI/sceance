This is a genuinely interesting problem, and you've already identified the core architectural gap correctly: the standard voice agent pipeline is **deaf to pronunciation**. STT throws away exactly the information you need — it normalizes "bonjour" spoken with a thick American accent and "bonjour" spoken by a native Parisian into the same text token. By the time the LLM sees it, the accent is gone. So the question is whether you can recover or capture that phonetic information somewhere in the pipeline.

The answer is yes, and there are several real approaches. Let me walk through them honestly, from simplest to most ambitious.

**The simplest approach that actually works: phoneme-level forced alignment.**

The idea is straightforward. You know what the user *should* have said (because you prompted them — "repeat after me: bonjour"). You have their raw audio. You run a forced alignment model that maps the audio onto the expected phoneme sequence and gives you a per-phoneme confidence score and timing. Where confidence is low, the pronunciation diverged. This doesn't require any exotic technology — tools like Montreal Forced Aligner, Kaldi, or even Whisper in timestamp mode can do this. The output is something like: "/b/ — good, /ɔ̃/ — weak, /ʒ/ — good, /u/ — substituted with /oʊ/, /ʁ/ — missing." That's enough for the agent to say "your 'on' nasal vowel needs work — you're saying it more like the English 'on' — and you're dropping the French R at the end."

This is viable to build today, and it fits into the Pipecat pipeline as a parallel branch: the audio goes to STT for understanding *what* they said, and simultaneously to an alignment/pronunciation scorer for understanding *how* they said it. The LLM gets both signals.

**The more sophisticated version: pronunciation scoring APIs.**

There are commercial APIs built exactly for this use case — language learning pronunciation assessment. The main ones worth knowing about are SpeechAce, ELSA's API, and Azure Speech Service's pronunciation assessment feature. Azure's is probably the most accessible for you since it's a major cloud provider, well-documented, and not a startup that might vanish. You send it audio plus the reference text, and it returns per-phoneme accuracy scores, an overall pronunciation score, and specific error categories (substitution, omission, insertion). It supports French. This is the "buy, don't build" answer, and for a prototype it's the right one.

The trade-off is latency. In a real-time voice conversation, you can't pause for 2 seconds while a pronunciation API scores the utterance before the agent responds. So the architecture needs to be **asynchronous**: the agent responds conversationally in real time (via the normal STT → LLM → TTS path), and the pronunciation assessment runs in parallel, with results injected into the *next* turn's context. So the agent might respond naturally to what you said, and then at the top of its next turn say "by the way, on that last sentence — your R in 'rouge' was too English, try it more from the back of the throat." That slight delay is actually natural for a language teacher — they often let you finish, respond to the content, then circle back to the pronunciation.

**The hardest part you mentioned: reproducing the user's accent and then voicing it correctly.**

This is the most ambitious piece and I want to be honest about where it stands. What you're describing is essentially: take the user's audio, extract a speaker embedding or prosodic profile that captures their accent, then generate two TTS outputs — one mimicking their accented version, one in correct native French — so the user can hear the contrast. "This is how you said it; this is how it should sound."

This is technically possible but pushing the edge of what's production-ready. Voice cloning (reproducing someone's voice/accent from a sample) exists — Cartesia, ElevenLabs, and others offer it. But cloning an *accent* specifically, as opposed to a voice timbre, and then producing a corrected version in the same voice but with native pronunciation — that's a research-grade task today, not an API call. The simpler and more effective version: the agent speaks the correct pronunciation in *its own* native French voice and says "listen to how I say it, then try again." That contrast — their attempt versus the model pronunciation — is what every language teacher does, and it doesn't require voice cloning at all. It just requires the TTS to be good at native French, which Cartesia is.

**What a realistic first version looks like:**

The agent is a French conversation partner. It speaks in French (or a mix, depending on level), prompts the user to speak, and responds naturally. In parallel, the audio runs through a pronunciation scorer — Azure or similar. The scores feed into the next turn's LLM context as structured data: "user's last utterance: pronunciation score 72/100, weak phonemes: /ʁ/ (substituted with English R), /y/ (substituted with /u/), nasal vowels inconsistent." The LLM, prompted to be a language coach, uses this to give targeted correction: "Your sentence was good — I understood everything. One thing to work on: when you say 'rue,' the French 'u' is not the same as English 'oo.' Push your lips forward, like you're about to whistle, and try again: 'rue.'" Then the TTS voices the correct pronunciation.

That's a genuinely useful product. It's not the full "hear your own accent played back corrected" vision, but it's the version that works with current tools and that a learner would actually find valuable.

**The real obstacles for this idea are not technical, they're product-shaped:**

Latency budget gets tight. You're now running STT plus pronunciation scoring plus LLM plus TTS, and the pronunciation scorer adds a round trip. For a voice conversation to feel natural, you need the total under about 1.5 seconds. The asynchronous design (score in parallel, deliver feedback next turn) solves this, but it means the feedback is always one turn behind.

The pedagogical design is harder than the engineering. What do you correct and what do you let go? A teacher who corrects every phoneme is exhausting and discouraging. A teacher who never corrects is useless. The prompting for the LLM needs a real pedagogical framework — something like "correct at most one pronunciation issue per exchange, prioritize errors that impede comprehension over accent details, and always acknowledge what they did well before correcting." That's a prompt-design problem, and it's the equivalent of what we just did with Inigo — you need to feed the model actual language-teaching methodology, not just "correct their pronunciation."

This is a solid idea. The technology exists, the integration path is clear, and the product gap in the market is real — most language apps either ignore pronunciation entirely or handle it as isolated word-repetition drills disconnected from conversation. A conversational agent that listens to *how* you speak, not just *what* you speak, during a real exchange — that's genuinely differentiated.
