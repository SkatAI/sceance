This is a beautiful problem — and it's *harder* than the accent correction case in ways that matter. Let me explain why, because it changes what's buildable.

**The pronunciation correction problem has a clear ground truth.** The phoneme /ʁ/ is either a French R or it isn't. You can score it. Poetry recitation has no single ground truth — there are many valid interpretations of how to voice a Baudelaire alexandrine, and "good" is a judgment, not a measurement. That shifts the entire technical approach.

What you're actually asking the system to evaluate is **prosody** — the melody of speech. That means intonation contour (pitch rising and falling), rhythm and timing (where you pause, which syllables you lengthen, how fast you go), emphasis and stress (which words carry weight), and dynamic range (loud vs soft, breathy vs full). A bored student reciting "Demain, dès l'aube, à l'heure où blanchit la campagne" reads it flat — monotone pitch, even timing, no pauses, every word at the same volume and speed. A good recitation of that same line has a specific shape: "Demain..." (pause, weight, this word carries the whole poem's grief) "...dès l'aube..." (lighter, moving forward) "...à l'heure où blanchit la campagne" (opening up, painting the image). The information is entirely in the *how*, not the *what*.

**Here's what's technically available to capture that.**

Pitch and timing extraction from audio is a solved problem — libraries like `parselmouth` (a Python wrapper around Praat, the standard phonetics tool) will give you an F0 pitch contour, intensity curve, and timing of every syllable from a raw audio file. This is not AI, it's signal processing — your PhD territory. You can extract these curves from both a reference recitation and the student's attempt and literally compare the shapes. Where the reference pitch rises and the student's stays flat, that's a missed intonation. Where the reference has a 400ms pause and the student has 50ms, that's a rushed transition.

So the basic architecture looks like this: you have a **reference recitation** of the poem — either pre-recorded by a human actor, or generated by your TTS with careful prompting for expressive delivery. You extract its prosodic profile (pitch contour, timing, intensity, pauses). The student records their attempt. You extract the same features. You align the two using dynamic time warping (DTW) — a classic technique for comparing two time series that may be at different speeds, and again, right in your signal processing wheelhouse. The DTW alignment gives you a per-segment similarity score: "your pitch on 'Demain' was flat where the reference rises," "you rushed through 'à l'heure où blanchit' without the pause after 'aube.'"

That comparison data feeds into the LLM as structured input, exactly like the phoneme scores in the language-learning case. The agent then gives coaching: "You read the line evenly, like a list. But Hugo is building toward something. Try putting real weight on 'Demain' — it's the first word and it carries all the longing. Then pause after 'dès l'aube,' let the silence do some work before you paint the landscape."

**Where it gets genuinely hard.**

The reference recitation is the key design decision. If you use a TTS-generated reference, you're limited by how expressive your TTS actually is. Cartesia is good, but "recite Hugo with soul" is a high bar — current TTS can do warm and natural, but the specific art of poetic recitation, the dramatic pacing and emotional coloring, is still at the edge of what TTS handles well. You'd want to test this honestly before committing: have Cartesia recite an alexandrine and listen critically. If it sounds like a good audiobook narrator, that might be enough. If it sounds like a slightly warm GPS, it's not.

The better but more labor-intensive approach: **curate human reference recordings** for each poem. A drama teacher, an actor, a poet — someone who knows how to voice these texts. That becomes your gold standard. It also becomes a content pipeline that doesn't scale for free. Every new poem needs a new reference. But for a high school context, the canon is finite — there are maybe fifty poems that French lycée students actually have to recite. That's a recordable corpus.

The second hard part is **what feedback to give.** Prosodic comparison gives you "your pitch was flat here, your timing was rushed there." But a student doesn't know what to do with "increase your F0 by 40Hz on syllable 7." The LLM's job is to translate the signal-level comparison into *acting direction*: "slow down here," "this word is the most important in the line, give it room," "you're reading this as if it's prose — find the caesura at the sixth syllable and let the line breathe there." That translation layer is where the system prompt does its work, and it needs real knowledge of French versification — what an alexandrine is, where the caesura falls, what enjambment does to pacing, how a volta works in a sonnet. Same lesson as Inigo: you need to feed the model the actual craft knowledge, not just "coach them to be more expressive."

**The third hard part is the most interesting one: French verse has structure.**

An alexandrine is not free verse. It has twelve syllables, a mandatory caesura after the sixth, and rules about mute-e that affect the count. A student who reads "Demain, dès l'aube, à l'heure où blanchit la campagne" needs to hit the rhythmic structure — 4/2 // 4/2 in the hémistiches — or the line collapses. This is *not* prosodic style, it's **metrical correctness**, and it's actually closer to the pronunciation-scoring problem: either you're counting the syllables right (including the mute-e rules) or you're not. A system that can check "did the student voice all twelve syllables, or did they swallow the mute-e in 'campagne' and only produce eleven?" — that's a real, verifiable assessment, and it's more tractable than scoring emotional expression. Start there.

**What a realistic v1 looks like:**

The agent has a library of poems with pre-recorded reference recitations (human or carefully-produced TTS). The student picks a poem. The agent recites a line or stanza as the model. The student repeats it. The system extracts prosody from both — pitch, timing, pauses, intensity — aligns them via DTW, and produces a structured comparison. The LLM, prompted with knowledge of French versification and acting direction, translates the comparison into one or two concrete coaching notes per attempt. The student tries again.

That loop — model, attempt, comparison, coaching, retry — is essentially a master class in miniature. And the voice agent format is genuinely better for this than a screen-based app, because poetry is an *oral* art. The student should be listening and speaking, not reading and clicking.

One thing I find genuinely compelling about this idea versus the language-learning one: the **market is narrow but deep.** Every French lycée student faces the oral exam. Teachers don't have time to coach every student's recitation individually. Parents can't help if they don't know the craft themselves. An agent that actually teaches you to *perform* a poem — not just memorize the words — fills a gap that is real, specific, and seasonal (exam prep peaks are predictable). It's also culturally very French in a way that might make it easier to position than a generic language app.

The signal processing part of this — F0 extraction, DTW alignment, prosodic comparison — is frankly closer to your PhD than to your Pipecat work. The interesting question is whether that excites you or whether you'd rather stay in the voice-agent-product lane and bolt on a pronunciation API instead of building the comparison engine yourself. Both are valid paths to a working product; they're different amounts of fun.
