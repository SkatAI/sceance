# Ignatian Discernment and the *Spiritual Exercises*: A Guide for the Newcomer

*Written for a reader of high cultural and philosophical literacy but little prior training in Christianity. Nothing here is simplified; where a theological term is unavoidable, it is defined the first time it appears.*

---

## 0. How to read this guide, and what kind of thing the *Exercises* are

Before any content, one orientation that prevents most early misunderstandings.

The *Spiritual Exercises* (*Ejercicios Espirituales*) is **not a book of doctrine and not a book meant to be read straight through**. It is a *manual* — a thin, deliberately unliterary handbook in 16th-century Spanish (with Latin interpolations) addressed not to the person undergoing the experience but to the person *guiding* it. Roland Barthes, in *Sade, Fourier, Loyola*, read it precisely as an *apparatus* — a machine for producing a certain kind of interior speech — and that is closer to the truth than treating it as devotional literature. What you "read" in the text is mostly a set of instructions, scaffolds, and timings. The actual content is generated inside the person doing the exercises, under guidance, over time.

The governing analogy is athletic, and Ignatius states it openly at the outset: *as walking, journeying, and running are exercises of the body, so methods of preparing and disposing the soul are exercises of the soul.* The point of an exercise is not to acquire information but to **change a capacity** — to make the will freer and the affectivity more perceptive. This places the *Exercises* squarely in the lineage Pierre Hadot reconstructed in *Philosophy as a Way of Life*: the ancient understanding of philosophy as *askēsis*, a regimen of self-transformation, of which the Ignatian text is a late, Christianized, and unusually systematic specimen. If you come to it as a philosopher, that is the most fruitful frame.

A second orientation, on vocabulary. Ignatius works with the inherited faculty psychology of his age — the soul has **memory**, **understanding** (intellect), and **will** — and with a cosmology of **"spirits"** (good and evil influences acting on the interior life). You do not have to share that metaphysics to take the practice seriously. Underneath the period idiom, what Ignatius is doing is a remarkably disciplined attention to *affective movements* — stirrings, impulses, attractions, repulsions, moods-with-a-direction — treated as **data that carry information about the orientation of a life**. That is the durable core. I will keep both registers visible: his language, and what it is doing.

## 1. The man and the moment

Íñigo López de Loyola was born around 1491 in the Basque country, the youngest son of a minor noble family. He was, by his own later account, a vain and ambitious courtier and soldier — given to *"the vanities of the world,"* dueling, dress, and the chivalric imagination. The hinge of his life is a wound. In 1521, defending the citadel of **Pamplona** against the French, a cannonball shattered his leg. The convalescence that followed, at the family castle of Loyola, was long, painful (he had the badly set leg re-broken and reset out of vanity, so that it would look well in courtier's hose), and tedious.

He asked for the chivalric romances he loved. There were none in the house. The only books were a ***Life of Christ*** (the *Vita Christi* of Ludolph of Saxony, a meditative harmony of the Gospels) and a collection of **lives of the saints** (a Spanish *Flos Sanctorum*, in the tradition of the *Golden Legend*). He read them to pass the time — and made the observation that would become the seed of everything.

He noticed that when he daydreamed of worldly glory and of serving a great lady, the fantasies delighted him *while he was in them*, but left him **dry, restless, and dissatisfied afterward**. When instead he imagined imitating the austere feats of Francis and Dominic, that too pleased him in the moment — but the pleasure *lasted*, leaving him **content and at peace**. The decisive move is in the word *afterward*. Ignatius attended not to the immediate pleasantness of an inner state but to its **aftertaste and its consequences over time**. This is, in embryo, the entire epistemology of Ignatian discernment: *the source and quality of an interior movement are read off its fruits, especially its durable fruits.* It is a pragmatist, "by their fruits you shall know them" criterion, applied to the inner life.

Recovered, he left for a kind of conversion-pilgrimage: a night-long vigil before the Black Madonna at **Montserrat**, where he laid down his sword (the chivalric gesture redirected); then nearly a year (1522–23) in the town of **Manresa**, living in austerity, begging, praying for hours, and passing through a harrowing crisis — a near-suicidal siege of **scruples** (obsessive, self-tormenting doubt about whether his sins were truly forgiven) from which he emerged with hard-won insight into how the interior enemy operates. At Manresa he also received intense mystical illuminations, the most famous on the bank of the river **Cardoner**, where, he said, he understood more in one sitting than in the rest of his life combined. The notes he kept of these experiences — what consoled, what desolated, what discerned true from false — are the raw material later worked up into the *Exercises*.

The rest, briefly: a pilgrimage to Jerusalem; a belated return to school (in his thirties, learning Latin grammar beside boys); studies at Alcalá and Salamanca, where his lay spiritual teaching twice drew the attention of the Inquisition; then the University of Paris, where he gathered the companions — Francis Xavier, Pierre Favre (Peter Faber), and others — who would, after vows at **Montmartre in 1534**, become the **Society of Jesus** (the Jesuits), papally approved in **1540**. He served as the first Superior General until his death in 1556. The *Exercises* received papal approval in **1548**. He was canonized in 1622.

Two contextual facts matter for reading the text. First, this is the era of the **Reformation and the Catholic Reform**: an age newly preoccupied with interiority, conscience, and the individual's relation to God, but also with discipline, method, and orthodoxy. Second, Ignatius is a **layman and a soldier**, not a theologian by formation. The *Exercises* have the character of field-tested procedure rather than speculative theology — which is exactly why they translate so well into a non-confessional, philosophical register.

## 2. The architecture: the "Four Weeks"

The *Exercises* are organized into **four "Weeks."** The word is misleading and you should disarm it immediately: a "Week" is **not seven days** but a *movement* or *phase* of the soul, of elastic length, adapted to the individual. The full enclosed retreat — the classic form — runs about **thirty days**. (Ignatius's **Annotation 19** also provides for making them "in daily life" over many months, an hour a day, without leaving one's occupations: this is the basis of the modern "retreat in daily life," and it is how most people undertake them today.)

The four Weeks map, loosely, onto the ancient threefold scheme of the spiritual life — the **purgative**, **illuminative**, and **unitive** ways — but Ignatius bends the scheme to his own dynamic. Before the First Week stands a prefatory meditation that functions as the keystone of the whole arch.

### 2.1 The First Principle and Foundation, and the concept of *indifference*

The **Principle and Foundation** (*Principio y Fundamento*) is the philosophical premise of everything that follows. Paraphrasing closely:

> The human being is created to praise, reverence, and serve God, and by this means to attain salvation. The other things on the face of the earth are created for the human being, to help in the pursuit of that end. From which it follows that one is to use them in so far as they help toward the end, and rid oneself of them in so far as they hinder it.

The technical phrase is *tantum quantum* — "**in so far as.**" Things are not good or bad simpliciter; they are to be weighed by their *function* relative to the end. From this Ignatius draws the most important — and most misread — term in his whole vocabulary: **indifference** (*indiferencia*).

> We ought to make ourselves *indifferent* to all created things, in so far as it is left to the liberty of our free will… so that, on our part, we do not want health more than sickness, riches more than poverty, honor more than dishonor, a long life more than a short one — and so in all the rest — desiring and choosing only what leads us more to the end for which we are created.

**This is the single concept a newcomer must get right.** *Indifference* here does **not** mean apathy, coldness, detachment-as-numbness, or "not caring." It means **interior freedom**: the condition of a will that is not *enslaved* by its own preferences, and that therefore *can* choose the better when it sees it. The Stoic resonance is real (compare *apatheia*), but the difference is decisive: this is not the extinction or quieting of desire but its **reordering toward a single end**. It is freedom-*for*, not freedom-*from*. A person of Ignatian indifference may have intense preferences; what they have surrendered is the *tyranny* of those preferences over the choice. Think of it as the equilibrium of a balance-scale that has been zeroed, so that it can register the true weight of the alternatives placed in it. (Ignatius will literally use the balance, the *fiel de balanza*, as an image later.)

Everything downstream — the meditations, the rules, the method of choosing — exists to *produce* this freedom and then to *use* it.

### 2.2 First Week — the disordered self, seen in the light of mercy

The **First Week** is the purgative phase. Its matter is **sin**: the history of sin (the angels, Adam, a single mortal sin damned forever) and, above all, **one's own** sin and disorder, contemplated unflinchingly. The classic exercises include a meditation on hell, using the **application of the senses** (see §3) — to *feel* the reality of a life turned away from its end.

A modern reader recoils here, expecting morbid guilt. That is a misreading of the *target affect*. The grace explicitly *asked for* is "shame and confusion," then "growing and intense sorrow and tears" — but the **deeper movement the Week is designed to produce is liberation through honesty in the presence of love.** Ignatius wants the retreatant to confront the disordered self *while standing inside an experience of being a loved sinner* — to look squarely at one's own capacity for self-betrayal and find oneself, against all expectation, held. The characteristic First Week colloquy ends not in despair but in **wonder and gratitude** — "Why am I still alive? Why is the world still bearing me?" — which converts, properly handled, into relief and freedom. If the Week produces only self-loathing, it has failed; the disorder must be seen *and surpassed by mercy*. This is, incidentally, where someone with a psychoanalytic formation will see the most: the First Week is a structured, time-limited descent into one's own disorder under the protection of a holding relation, undertaken precisely so that the material can be *worked* rather than merely suffered.

### 2.3 Second Week — the life of Christ, the call, and the great meditations

The **Second Week** turns from the self to a person: it is a sustained **imaginative contemplation of the life of Jesus**, from the Incarnation through the public ministry. The dominant prayer-mode shifts from discursive meditation (thinking *about*) to **contemplation** (imaginatively *being present to* a scene — see §3). The grace asked is *interior knowledge of the Lord, who became human for me, that I may love him more and follow him more closely.* Note the form of the petition: not knowledge *that* but knowledge *of* — an affective, personal acquaintance — and explicitly *for the sake of* love and following.

Embedded in this Week are five "set-piece" exercises that are the structural heart of the entire *Exercises*. They are not contemplations of Gospel scenes but constructed meditations designed to **probe and free the will** before the great choice. Take them slowly; they are the most distinctive thing Ignatius made.

**(a) The Call of the King (*El Reino*, the Kingdom).** A parable in two movements. First, imagine a noble earthly king, manifestly worthy, who summons everyone to join a great and costly enterprise, sharing fully in its hardships so as to share in its victory; *what would it mean to refuse such a leader?* Then, the *a fortiori*: Christ the eternal King issues the same summons to the whole world. The exercise introduces the **magis** — "*the more*" — the orientation that defines Ignatian spirituality: those who wish *to distinguish themselves* offer not only their whole selves to the labor but desire even to **share the leader's poverty and reproach**, if that should serve. The *magis* is the restless reaching toward "what is more conducive to the end." (The Jesuit motto *Ad Maiorem Dei Gloriam* — "to the greater glory of God," AMDG — is its institutional form: not "the glory of God" but the *greater*.)

**(b) The Meditation on Two Standards (*Dos Banderas*).** A *standard* is a battle-flag, hence an allegiance. Ignatius asks the retreatant to imagine two camps and to discern the **deep strategy** of each. The strategy of "Lucifer" — the enemy — proceeds in a sequence: he tempts first to **riches** (covetousness), thence to the **honor** of the world, thence to swelling **pride**, "and from these three steps he leads to all the other vices." The strategy of Christ proceeds in the inverse sequence: he draws toward **poverty** (at least of spirit, and, if it should be chosen, actual poverty), thence to a willingness to bear **reproach and contempt**, thence to **humility**, "and from these three steps to all the other virtues." This is one of the most penetrating things in the book: it is a structural anatomy of **how disorder and grace each propagate** through a personality — each by a characteristic *cascade*. The point is not poverty as deprivation but the discernment of which cascade one's desires are entering.

**(c) The Three Classes (or Types) of Persons (*Tres Binarios*).** A meditation on the **will's self-deception**. Three persons have each acquired a large sum of money by means that were not entirely pure; each genuinely wishes to be rid of the *disordered attachment* to it in order to be at peace with God. The **first** wishes to remove the attachment but never gets around to taking any actual means; they die having done nothing. The **second** will do *anything to be rid of the attachment* — *except* the one thing that would touch the money itself; they bend God's will to fit their predetermined conclusion. The **third** wants neither to keep nor to dispose of the money as such; they want *only what God wills*, having attained genuine indifference toward the concrete thing. The exercise's sharpness is its **concreteness**: it is easy to be indifferent in the abstract and impossible in the particular case, and the meditation forces the retreatant to test their freedom against the *actual* attachment, not a hypothetical one.

**(d) The Three Kinds (Degrees) of Humility (*Tres Maneras de Humildad*).** A scale of configurations of desire toward Christ. The **first** degree: one would never, for anything, sin gravely against God. The **second**: one has reached such indifference that one would not even commit a *minor* deliberate sin, and is poised in equilibrium between riches and poverty, honor and dishonor — the full freedom of the *Foundation*. The **third** degree is the radical one, and it is the affective summit of the *Exercises*: *in order to imitate Christ more closely*, and provided it be equally for the service and praise of God, one **positively chooses and desires** poverty with the poor Christ rather than riches, reproach with the reviled Christ rather than honor, and to be **accounted a useless fool** for Christ's sake rather than wise and prudent in the world's eyes. This is not masochism and not a recommendation to seek suffering; it is the description of a love that has reconfigured the structure of *preference* itself, so that nearness to the beloved outranks the goods one previously ranked first. Ignatius offers it as something to *pray for the desire of*, knowing it cannot be willed directly.

**(e) The Election (*Elección*) — the choice.** With the will thus exercised and freed, the retreatant comes to the practical center: a concrete **decision** — historically, a choice of "state of life" (vocation), but in modern practice any significant, freely-disposable life-decision, or a reform of life within a state already fixed. The *method* of choosing is important enough to have its own section below (§5).

### 2.4 Third Week — the Passion, and the testing of the choice

The **Third Week** contemplates the **Passion**: the suffering and death of Jesus, scene by scene, with the grace asked being *sorrow, compassion, and shame* — "to be sorrowful with Christ in sorrow," to suffer *with* (the literal sense of *com-passion*). Structurally, this Week **confirms the Election**: a choice made in the relative light and consolation of the Second Week is now carried into the contemplation of cost and darkness. If the choice holds — if it can be lived alongside the suffering Christ without dissolving — that is one of the surest signs of its rightness. The Week is, in effect, a **stress-test of the decision** under the affect of loss.

### 2.5 Fourth Week — the Resurrection, and the Contemplation to Attain Love

The **Fourth Week** contemplates the **Resurrection** and the appearances of the risen Christ. The grace asked is, distinctively, *joy* — "to rejoice intensely at the great glory and joy of Christ." Ignatius notes that here one contemplates Christ exercising the **"office of consoler,"** comforting his friends as friends console one another; the affect proper to this Week is shared gladness.

The *Exercises* culminate in the **Contemplation to Attain Love** (*Contemplatio ad Amorem*; *Contemplación para Alcanzar Amor*), often simply "the Contemplatio." It opens with two premises that you should read as a small philosophy of love: first, that **love shows itself in deeds more than in words**; second, that **love consists in mutual communication** — the lover gives and shares what he has with the beloved, and vice versa; love is reciprocal exchange. Then four "points," a movement of widening and deepening attention:

1. **Recall the benefits received** — of creation, of redemption, and the particular gifts of one's own life — and consider, with great affection, how much the giver has given.
2. **Consider how God dwells in creatures** — in the elements giving existence, in plants giving growth, in animals giving sensation, in human beings giving understanding — and *in me*, making me a temple.
3. **Consider how God labors and works** *for me* in all created things — *operatio* — God as active, working in the heavens, elements, plants, fruits, on my behalf.
4. **Consider how all good things descend from above** — as rays descend from the sun, as waters from their fountain — every good and gift flowing down from the supreme source.

Interspersed is the prayer that crystallizes the whole spirituality, the **Suscipe** ("Receive"):

> Take, Lord, and receive all my liberty, my memory, my understanding, and my entire will — all that I have and possess. You gave it to me; to you, Lord, I return it. All is yours; dispose of it wholly according to your will. Give me only your love and your grace; that is enough for me.

Notice that the surrender is offered in the exact terms of the **faculty psychology** that underlies the whole text — *memory, understanding, will*, the three powers of the soul — and that what is asked in return is not any created good but *love and grace alone*, which "is enough." The Contemplatio is what gives Ignatian spirituality its signature orientation, the phrase by which it is best known: **finding God in all things**, and its complementary ideal of the **"contemplative in action"** — a contemplation that does not withdraw from the world but discovers the divine *within* engagement with it. This is why the *Exercises* end not in retreat but in *return*: the entire apparatus is meant to send the person back into ordinary life with reordered desire and a trained capacity to perceive.

## 3. The method of prayer: how the exercises are actually performed

The Weeks supply *what* to contemplate; a set of techniques supplies *how*. These are worth understanding even apart from belief, as a genuine technology of attention and imagination.

**The Preludes.** Each exercise opens with a fixed scaffold. After a brief **preparatory prayer**, one makes a **composition of place** (*composición viendo el lugar*) — an act of *imaginative construction*: one builds the scene in concrete sensory detail (the road to Bethlehem, the upper room, the hill of Calvary), placing oneself bodily within it. Then one asks for the **specific grace desired** for *this* exercise — the *id quod volo*, "what I want and desire." This last point is structurally crucial and easy to miss: **one never prays generically.** Each exercise has its *own* petition — sorrow here, intimate knowledge there, joy elsewhere — and the precision of the asking shapes the whole. Discernment begins with knowing what, precisely, you are seeking.

**Imaginative contemplation.** In the Second through Fourth Weeks, the dominant mode is not analysis but *presence*: one enters a Gospel scene with the imagination — watching the persons, hearing what they say, observing what they do — and lets oneself be affected. This is a cognitive-affective instrument: by engaging the imagination rather than the discursive intellect, it works on the level where desire is actually formed. (Here the resonance with depth psychology is unavoidable and was not lost on twentieth-century Jesuits.)

**The Application of the Senses (*aplicación de sentidos*).** A distinct, more *receptive* mode, usually used as a repetition at the end of a day's exercises. One applies the five "interior senses" to the mystery in turn — seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, touching the scene — gently, with little reasoning, letting the scene saturate the affectivity. It is the most contemplative and least effortful of the modes.

**Repetition (*repetición*).** A core principle, and a counterintuitive one for the productivity-minded: one **returns** to the points of a previous exercise — not to *cover more material* but to dwell where one was *most moved*, whether by consolation, desolation, or insight. Ignatius's governing instruction (Annotation 2): it is not abundance of knowledge that satisfies the soul but **"sentir y gustar las cosas internamente"** — *to feel and taste things interiorly*. Where you found something, **stay** there; do not rush on. Discernment requires *dwelling*.

**The Colloquy (*coloquio*).** Each exercise ends in *conversation* — speaking to Christ, or to Mary, or to the Father, "**as one friend speaks to another**," or as a servant to a master, intimately and concretely, about what has arisen. The colloquy is where the contemplated material is *addressed* and *responded to*.

## 4. Discernment of Spirits: the heart of the matter

We now reach the part you named first, and the most original. Within the text, the discernment material appears as two appended sets of **"Rules for perceiving and knowing in some way the diverse movements caused in the soul"** — the *Reglas para sentir y conocer las mociones*. The key word is **moción**, *movement/motion*: an affective stirring — an attraction, a heaviness, a surge of energy or its draining away, a thought that arrives with a charge of feeling. Ignatius's premise is that these movements are **not noise but signal**: they have *sources* and *directions*, and they can be *read*.

Two affective states are defined with care and become the basic instruments of measurement.

**Consolation** (*consolación*) is any interior movement by which the soul is **inflamed with love of God**; any increase of **faith, hope, and love**; any interior **joy that draws toward heavenly things** and the soul's true end; tears that flow from love; and, in general, the settling of the soul into **quiet and peace in its Creator**. The mark is an *increase in self-transcending orientation* and a *durable peace*.

**Desolation** (*desolación*) is the contrary: **darkness and disturbance** of soul, a pull toward "low and earthly things," restlessness from various agitations and temptations, a movement *toward distrust, hopelessness, lovelessness*, finding oneself "slothful, tepid, sad, and as if separated" from one's Creator. The mark is *contraction, turning-in-on-self, and a draining of faith-hope-love.*

Crucially, **neither is to be equated with mere pleasantness or unpleasantness.** A consolation can be painful (sorrow for one's sins is a consolation; tears can console); a desolation can be superficially agreeable (the seductive ease of giving up). The criterion is **direction**, not hedonic tone — whether the movement *opens the soul toward its end and toward love, or closes it.*

Ignatius then gives **two sets of rules**, calibrated to two stages of the spiritual life. This staging is itself a sophisticated point: *the same interior phenomenon means different things depending on where the person is*, and the rules for a beginner are inadequate — even dangerous — for someone further along.

### 4.1 Rules of the First Week (the fourteen rules)

These suit the **purgative** stage — someone moving *out of a disordered life toward order*, where the spirits act relatively bluntly. The most important among them:

1. **Rule 1 & 2 — the tactics reverse with one's direction.** For a person sliding *from bad to worse*, the enemy proposes pleasures and the good spirit "stings the conscience" with remorse. For a person moving *from good to better*, the polarity flips: the enemy now harasses with **anxiety, false sadness, and specious obstacles** to discourage progress, while the good spirit gives **courage, strength, consolation, tears, and peace.** The lesson: you cannot interpret a movement without knowing the *vector* of the life it is acting on.

2. **Rule 3 & 4 — the definitions** of consolation and desolation given above.

3. **Rule 5 — in desolation, never make a change.** *In time of desolation, hold to the resolutions you made in the time of preceding consolation.* This is the single most practically useful rule in the entire corpus, and it generalizes far beyond religion: **do not make major decisions, or revise commitments, from inside a contracted and lightless state**, because in desolation one is not counseled by the better spirit. Decide in clarity; *endure* the dark without re-deciding.

4. **Rule 6 — act against it (*agere contra*).** In desolation, lean *deliberately against* the pull: intensify prayer, examination, a fitting penance — do the opposite of what the heaviness wants, in order to break its grip. *Agere contra* — "to act against" — is a general Ignatian instrument: when you detect a disordered attachment or a desolate slackening, *tilt the other way* to restore freedom.

5. **Rules 7, 8 — endurance and patience.** Remember that consolation will return; the desolation is a passing trial; meet it with patience.

6. **Rule 9 — the three reasons for desolation.** Why is the experience permitted? (a) Because of our own *tepidity and negligence* — we have let consolation lapse and it is partly deserved; (b) as a *trial* — to test how much we are worth and how far we will extend ourselves in service *without the wages of felt consolation*; (c) for *true self-knowledge* — to teach us, by its withdrawal, that consolation is a **gift** and not a possession we can command, and so to keep us humble. The third reason is the most psychologically acute: desolation as the pedagogy of the *gratuity* of grace.

7. **Rules 10, 11 — the counter-cyclical disciplines.** In consolation, *store up strength* for the desolation to come; in desolation, *recall* that with grace one can resist. In consolation, humble oneself (do not over-attribute it to one's own merit); in desolation, take heart. A deliberate counter-weighting against the natural tendency of each state to believe it is permanent.

8. **Rules 12, 13, 14 — the phenomenology of the enemy.** Three images. (12) The enemy is *weak when faced and strong when tolerated*: resist firmly at the first onset and he collapses; hesitate and he grows. (13) The enemy behaves like a **false lover who craves secrecy**: he wants his suggestions hidden, and is undone the moment they are *spoken aloud* to a wise confidant. The **pull toward secrecy is itself a diagnostic sign** — what cannot bear to be brought into the light of an honest conversation is, by that very fact, suspect. (14) The enemy attacks at the **weakest point**, like a commander besieging the least-defended wall — so one must know, and fortify, one's own most vulnerable place.

### 4.2 Rules of the Second Week (the eight rules)

These are *"for greater discernment of spirits,"* suited to the **illuminative** stage — a person well-disposed and progressing, against whom the crude tactics no longer work. Here the enemy changes strategy entirely, and the rules become subtle. They are the more philosophically interesting set.

The central insight is the **"angel of light"** (*sub angelo lucis*): against a good soul, the enemy no longer tempts toward the obviously bad — that would be detected at once — but **disguises himself as a source of light**, proposing *genuinely good and holy thoughts* in order to lead the soul, by imperceptible steps, toward his own ends. The danger is no longer crude pleasure but **plausible, pious, apparently virtuous suggestion**.

This generates Ignatius's most distinctive analytic move:

- **Examine the whole *sequence* of thoughts (*el discurso de los pensamientos*).** (Rules 4–5.) Because the enemy *begins* with the good (entering through the soul's own holy dispositions), you cannot judge a movement by its **beginning** alone. You must trace its **whole course — beginning, middle, and end.** If the entire trajectory is good and leads to the good, it is from the good spirit. But if, in its course, the thread of thought **ends in something evil, or distracting, or *less good* than what the soul had first proposed**, or if it **weakens, disquiets, or disturbs** the soul — robbing it of the peace it had — *that is the sign of the evil spirit.* Discernment, on this account, is **diachronic and narrative**: you read the *whole arc of a movement*, especially its **"tail" (its end-state)**, and you read it backward. This is a genuinely sophisticated epistemology of motive — it locates the truth of an inner movement not in its felt quality at any instant but in its *temporal shape and terminus*.

- **Once detected by its bad end, retrace the whole course** (Rule 6) — to learn the enemy's *characteristic tactic* against *you*, so as to recognize and refuse it earlier next time. Discernment accumulates self-knowledge.

- **The texture of the touch differs with one's direction** (Rule 7). For a soul progressing *from good to better*, the **good spirit touches it gently, lightly, sweetly — "like a drop of water entering a sponge"** — while the evil spirit enters **sharply, with noise and disturbance — "like water falling on stone."** (For a soul going the other way, reversed.) The criterion is the *manner* of the movement's arrival: ease and quiet *consonant with the soul's direction* versus jarring violence against it. The image is exact and worth keeping: a true movement, in a well-disposed person, does not clatter.

- **Consolation without preceding cause** (*sin causa precedente*) (Rules 2, 8) — the most philosophically dense point in all of Ignatius, and the one Karl Rahner built an entire interpretation upon. Ordinarily a consolation has a *cause*: some prior thought, perception, or act of the will *occasions* it, and where there is such a mediating cause, **either spirit can be its author** (the good for the good, the enemy disguised as light for ill) — so it must be discerned. But Ignatius identifies one case as the unique privilege of **God alone**: a consolation arriving with **no preceding object or act of will to occasion it** — the soul finding itself drawn wholly into love with nothing it can point to as the trigger. *This* alone, says Ignatius, comes immediately and unmistakably from the Creator, "who can enter the soul, leave it, and move it, drawing it wholly into love of his Divine Majesty." It is the one experience treated as immune to the deception of mediated motive — the bedrock touchstone.

- **And yet** — Rule 8, the crucial caution — even here, vigilance does not cease. The *moment of* consolation-without-cause is divine; but in the **period immediately following**, when the soul, still warm and "fervent," **forms its own plans, conclusions, and resolutions**, those are *no longer directly from God*. They are the soul's *own* additions — into which its habits, and the enemy, can slip. So the **"afterglow" must itself be examined** before it is acted on. The structure is remarkable: Ignatius grants exactly one experience of immediate certainty, and then immediately fences off its *consequences* from that certainty. Nothing — not even the surest consolation — exempts the resulting *decisions* from discernment.

If you want to feel the intellectual seriousness of the discernment tradition, dwell on those last two rules together. They constitute a genuine and self-aware epistemology of inner experience: a single point of immediate self-evidence, surrounded on all sides by the requirement of mediated, retrospective, narrative testing — including the testing of what one concludes from the self-evident point itself.

## 5. Discernment of Election: how to make a sound decision

The discernment of *spirits* serves a practical end: the discernment of a *choice*. Ignatius lays out **three "times" (modes, *tiempos*)** in which a sound election can be made. They are ordered by *clarity*, and the third — the ordinary case — comes with explicit method.

**First time.** When God so moves and draws the will that the soul, **without doubting or being able to doubt**, simply *follows* — the paradigm cases being sudden, total conversions (Paul on the road; Matthew rising from the tax-table at a word). Immediate, certain, requiring no deliberation. It is **rare**, and one cannot manufacture it.

**Second time.** When **clarity comes through the experience of consolations and desolations over time** — when, by living with the alternatives and attending to where consolation consistently settles and where desolation gathers, "much light and understanding" is derived through the discernment of spirits. This is the **proper home of the discernment of spirits**: the decision is read off the *pattern* of interior movements across time. Note again the diachronic character — one does not consult a single feeling but watches a *trend*.

**Third time.** A **"tranquil time"** (*tiempo tranquilo*), when the soul is *not* being pulled this way and that by different spirits and so can use its **natural faculties freely and calmly.** For this most common situation Ignatius gives **two complementary methods**:

*The first method* (a six-step rational deliberation):
1. **State the matter** to be decided, clearly.
2. **Hold the indifference** of the *Foundation* — stand in equilibrium, "like the pointer of a balance" (*fiel de balanza*), not yet inclined to either side, wanting only the end.
3. **Ask God to move the will** toward what is more for his service and praise.
4. **Weigh the advantages and disadvantages** of each option, *by reason* — soberly, on both sides.
5. **See which way reason inclines** — decide according to the *weightier rational motives*, not according to inclination.
6. **Offer the chosen option to God** and seek *confirmation* of it (which the Third and Fourth Weeks then supply).

*The second method* (four imaginative tests of motive, to be used as checks on the first):
1. **Check the source of the love** prompting the choice — does it *descend from* love of God, or rise from some lesser, self-serving attachment? The good choice's motive comes "from above."
2. **The stranger test.** Imagine a person you have never met, whom you wish to advise perfectly; *what would you counsel them* in this case? Then take your own advice. (A device for *distance* from one's own interest.)
3. **The deathbed test.** Imagine yourself **at the point of death**: which choice would you then *wish* you had made? Choose that.
4. **The judgment test.** Imagine yourself **at the Last Judgment**, before God: which choice would you then wish to have made? Choose that.

The two methods together are a small masterpiece of practical reason: a *rational* weighing (method one) cross-checked by a set of *imaginative displacements* designed to neutralize self-deception by shifting the standpoint — to another person, to one's deathbed, to ultimate accountability. The deathbed and stranger tests in particular have an obvious secular utility and are still taught, stripped of their theology, as decision-making heuristics.

A standing constraint frames all of this: the **"first time" certainty cannot be forced, and the others require that the matter be one the will is genuinely free to choose** (one does not "discern" whether to honor a binding prior commitment — that is settled). And Ignatius insists, against the second-class-of-persons temptation, that one must enter the deliberation in *real* indifference, not having secretly pre-decided and then sought a pious rationale.

## 6. The Examen: the daily instrument

If the thirty-day *Exercises* are the intensive, the **Examen** is the daily maintenance practice — and Ignatius regarded it as the **one exercise never to be omitted**, even when everything else lapses. He distinguishes the **particular examen** (concentrated effort against *one* specific fault, tracked over the day) from the **general examen** of the whole day. The classic general form has **five points**:

1. **Give thanks** to God for the benefits received this day.
2. **Ask for grace** to know one's sins — and, more broadly, one's *interior movements* — and to be rid of them.
3. **Review the day**, hour by hour, examining one's thoughts, words, and deeds — and the *movements* that ran beneath them.
4. **Ask pardon** for what was amiss.
5. **Resolve amendment** with God's grace, and look ahead to the day to come.

Twentieth-century Jesuit writers (notably George Aschenbrenner, in a much-cited essay) reframed this as the **"Examen of Consciousness"** rather than of *conscience* — shifting the emphasis from **moral bookkeeping** (a tally of faults) to **daily discernment**: a review of *where God was present in the day*, where one felt drawn and where resistant, where consolation and desolation arose and what they were pointing to. On this reading the Examen is the *discernment of spirits practiced in miniature, every day* — the habit that, over time, trains the perceptual capacity the great rules describe. For a newcomer, this is the single most portable practice in the whole tradition: a structured, ten-minute, daily *attention to the affective texture and direction of one's own experience*, which one can undertake with as much or as little metaphysics as one chooses to bring.

## 7. A few load-bearing concepts, gathered

For reference, the terms that recur and carry the most weight:

- **Indifference (*indiferencia*)** — interior freedom; the un-enslaved will, poised to choose the better. *Not* apathy. (§2.1)
- ***Tantum quantum*** — "in so far as": created things used to the degree they serve the end, set aside to the degree they hinder it. (§2.1)
- **The *magis* / AMDG** — "the more"; the orientation toward the *greater* good and glory, never merely the sufficient. (§2.3a)
- ***Agere contra*** — "to act against": deliberately leaning opposite to a disordered pull, to restore freedom. (§4.1)
- **Consolation / Desolation** — the two read-able affective vectors; expansion-toward-love versus contraction-toward-self. Judged by *direction*, not pleasantness. (§4)
- **The sequence of thoughts (*el discurso*)** — the rule that a movement is judged by its whole arc and its end, not its beginning. (§4.2)
- **Consolation without preceding cause** — the one immediate, undeceivable divine touch — and the caution that its *aftermath* is not. (§4.2)
- **Finding God in all things / the contemplative in action** — the resulting stance: not withdrawal but reordered engagement with the world. (§2.5)
- **The Presupposition (Annotation 22)** — a meta-rule of charity governing the whole relation between director and retreatant (and, by extension, any honest interlocutor): *be readier to put a good construction on another's statement than to condemn it*; and where it seems wrong, ask how they mean it before judging. A principle of interpretive charity, stated four centuries before the philosophers gave it that name.
- **Adaptation / *cura personalis*** — Annotations 18–20: the *Exercises* are to be **adapted to the individual** — their disposition, capacity, education, time. There is no one-size delivery; the director fits the instrument to the person. This is why the text is a manual for a *guide*, and why the same exercises run very differently for different people.

## 8. Intellectual coordinates (for the philosophically trained reader)

A few connections that may help you situate the material, offered as suggestions rather than the tradition's own self-understanding:

- **As *askēsis*.** Pierre Hadot's recovery of ancient philosophy as *spiritual exercise* — a regimen aimed at the transformation of the self rather than the accumulation of doctrine — gives the *Exercises* their most natural philosophical home. Hadot himself drew the line. Read this way, Ignatius belongs less with systematic theology than with the practical-transformative lineage running from the Stoic and Epicurean exercises through to him.

- **As a phenomenology of affect.** Strip the cosmology of spirits, and what remains is a disciplined claim that **affective movements are cognitively significant** — that moods, attractions, and aversions carry information about the orientation of a life and can be *read* with method. The criteria — direction over hedonic tone, the whole arc over the instant, the end over the beginning, the durable fruit over the momentary feel — constitute a small, coherent epistemology of inner experience. Compare and contrast at will with phenomenological treatments of mood and intentional feeling.

- **As a technology of desire.** The great Second-Week meditations (Two Standards, Three Classes, Three Degrees) are not arguments; they are *operations on the will*, attempts to **reconfigure the structure of preference** through imaginative rehearsal. Anyone with a psychoanalytic formation will recognize the terrain — the working-through of attachment, the displacement of the standpoint, the holding relation of the First Week — and the question of *whose desire* one is finally enacting. Roland Barthes, in *Sade, Fourier, Loyola*, read the *Exercises* as a self-enclosed *language* — a system for generating an "interlocution" with a deliberately produced addressee — and the Jesuit historian-theorist **Michel de Certeau** wrote with great subtlety on Ignatian experience and its writing. Both are worth reading precisely *against* a purely devotional reading, and both are accessible entry points from a secular, French-intellectual formation.

- **A caution about the comparison.** None of these mappings is the tradition's own self-understanding, and each loses something. The "by their fruits" epistemology, the deathbed and judgment tests, the *magis*, the Suscipe — all are oriented, in their native frame, toward a *real Other* and a real end (God, salvation, love), not toward self-optimization. One can profitably borrow the instruments without the metaphysics; one should at least be aware that, in doing so, one is removing the keystone the architecture was built to carry.

## 9. How to actually engage with this, and what to read

**The text itself.** Begin with a good translation *with apparatus*, not the bare text.
- In English: **David L. Fleming, *Draw Me Into Your Friendship*** prints a literal translation and a contemporary paraphrase side by side and is the gentlest serious entry. **Louis J. Puhl** is the classic readable English version. **George E. Ganss**'s translation and commentary (and his Classics of Western Spirituality volume, *Ignatius of Loyola: Spiritual Exercises and Selected Works*) is the scholarly standard.
- In French: **the Jean-Claude Guy translation** (*Exercices spirituels*, Seuil, coll. Points-Sagesses) is the standard accessible critical edition; the **Édouard Gueydan / "groupe de Lyon"** translation is the other major reference. Pair it with the **Autobiography** — *Le Récit du pèlerin* — and, for the advanced, the **Spiritual Diary**.

**Commentary on the discernment rules** (where a newcomer most wants help):
- **Timothy M. Gallagher, *The Discernment of Spirits* and *Spiritual Consolation*** — patient, practical, rule-by-rule; the best first companion.
- **Jules J. Toner, *A Commentary on St. Ignatius' Rules for the Discernment of Spirits*** — the most rigorous *analytic* treatment, well suited to a philosophically demanding reader who wants the distinctions pressed hard.
- **Michael Ivens, *Understanding the Spiritual Exercises*** — a careful, paragraph-by-paragraph scholarly commentary on the whole text.

**The wider tradition and its modern interpretation:**
- **Karl Rahner**, on *consolation without preceding cause* and the "logic of existential decision" (e.g., *The Dynamic Element in the Church*) — the most ambitious philosophical-theological reading.
- **William A. Barry**, *Finding God in All Things* and *Letting God Come Close* — the Ignatian *experience* and the dynamics of spiritual direction, accessibly.
- **Harvey D. Egan**, *The Spiritual Exercises and the Ignatian Mystical Horizon* — the mystical theology underneath.

**On the Examen specifically:** George Aschenbrenner's essay *"Consciousness Examen"* (widely reprinted) is the short, decisive reframing.

**The philosophical/secular angle:** **Pierre Hadot, *Philosophy as a Way of Life*** (for *askēsis*); **Roland Barthes, *Sade, Fourier, Loyola*** (for the *Exercises* as language-system); and **Michel de Certeau**'s essays on Ignatian and mystical experience.

**A practical note on *doing* rather than reading.** The tradition's own conviction is that the *Exercises* are *made*, not studied — and that they require a **guide** (a trained spiritual director) and **adaptation to the person** (Annotations 18–20). The ordinary modern forms are the **"19th-Annotation" retreat in daily life** (some months, with a weekly meeting with a director) and the residential **eight-day** or **thirty-day** retreats at Jesuit houses. The two most portable practices to begin *now*, alone, are the **daily Examen** (§6) and, when facing a real decision, the **two methods of the third time** (§5) — both of which yield something even when undertaken with philosophical reserve about the metaphysics.

---

*A closing word on stance. The deepest mistake a sophisticated newcomer can make is to treat this as a system of beliefs to be assessed for truth. It is, first, a set of practices to be assessed for what they do — and what they claim to do is to make a person interiorly freer, more perceptive about the texture and direction of their own affective life, and more capable of choosing well. Whether one shares the metaphysics or not, that is a serious and testable claim, and the right way to meet a discipline of that kind is the way one meets any askēsis: not by adjudicating its creed from outside, but by trying its instruments and watching, over time, what fruit they bear. Which is, of course, exactly the criterion Ignatius started from on his sickbed in Loyola.*
